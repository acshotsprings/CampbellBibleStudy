Freedom from Condemnation — Get Back in the Race (A Common Purpose, Msg 2)

Pastor Matt Scully, Trinity Church
Second message in the A Common Purpose series. No title was announced from the pulpit; logged under a descriptive title. Note on what this message actually is: Pastor Matt opened by closing out last week’s sermon, then set aside the message he had prepared for this week and preached an unplanned exhortation instead — “the Holy Spirit just constantly frustrates me by doing things that weren’t on my schedule, and then I have to wrestle with it.” He announced that the prepared sermon moves to next week and that the research for it is already done. Ran 22 minutes, ending just before noon — short by his standards and he said so: “if you only knew how long you could be here if I said all that I wanted to say. That is why we do series.” Big idea: conviction about how you have spent your life is not the same thing as condemnation for it — repent, move forward, and then move on, and get back into the calling you think you forfeited. Series framing — what “common purpose” means: each of us has an individual life and an individual purpose, but there is a commonality in living in community. He contrasted the American pattern (work, home, close the door, TV, bed, repeat) with cultures where families change clothes after work and spend the evening outside together breaking bread. His point: our culture makes it hard to see how my life fits with your life, and when the pieces rub wrong the easy move is to withdraw and keep company only with people we already get along with. “That is not the way that the church was built. We were built to do life with each other.” Recap of last week — “Possessing Eternity” (Sept 6, entry #24 — partial recording; this recap is the only record of that message’s body): the first commonality is eternity — every human being will face it, and you are already living it now; this life is simply the part of your eternity that happens on earth. 1 Cor 3:12–13 — gold, silver and precious stones versus wood, hay and straw; each man’s work becomes evident because the fire tests its quality. Work done with God’s kingdom and eternity in view survives; work lived for the pleasure of the moment burns. His challenge, restated: what happens if we arrive in eternity saved but with nothing left — everything we lived for consumed? He noted the text says a man may suffer loss and yet himself be saved, though as through fire (v.15). The eternal filter: the temporal is not bad, it just cannot be the thing. “Is my life being given to temporal satisfactions or eternal glory?” Run the decision through eternity and it changes how you lead a family, how you are a husband or wife, how you do the job you are paid for. Self-deprecating aside: he had gone to the lake Monday with three boats and two of them broke down — “the temporal was not as fun in that moment as I had hoped.” Coaching illustration: back when he was a youth pastor coaching girls’ basketball and volleyball, most of those players ended up in his youth group; winning was the goal but never the point. “Who cares if we go undefeated on the basketball court, but those girls enter hell for eternity? And I failed.” Restated deliberately: even if I succeed at what man looks at, if I have not offered them eternity I have failed them. The Esau turn — and the correction to last week: he retold Esau trading the birthright for a bowl of soup (Gen 25:29–34) and said plainly that at some point every one of us has been Esau — given in to a moment of urge or compulsion and walked away carrying the consequences. Then the pastoral correction he said he owed the room: “a sermon like last week could really heap on a lot of condemnation and shame — not the heart of God.” Rom 8:1 — there is now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus. “While we needed to be convicted last week, we did not need to be shamed or condemned.” Paired with Phil 3:13–14, forgetting what lies behind and pressing on toward the upward call, which he quoted twice and treated as the operative instruction. The felon analogy — the spine of the message: he guessed aloud that people in the room carry felonies, and headed off any judgment: “you’re sitting in a room full of people who would have felonies on their record if they had been caught.” He walked the practical machinery honestly — loss of firearms, parole officer, probation, voting rights — and then the job interview where the first question is “are you a felon?” and the honest answer ends the conversation. He granted these are real consequences of real decisions. Then the turn: “the church is just as guilty of doing it.” We keep reminding people who are trying to get into a new life of how they failed, and we never let them step into what God has called them to be. “We’ve got our felons in church” — that’s the guy who committed adultery, that’s the one who sold drugs — carrying a stigma at church when what God said is that you are a new creation (2 Cor 5:17). Two restoration cases: Paul — a murderer of Christians who repented and then wrote much of the New Testament; “they tried to put him in church jail, but he didn’t stay…he said, give me a pen and some paper, I got something to say.” Peter — after denying Jesus he gets one question, “do you love Me?”, and a commission, “feed My sheep” (John 21:15–17). “Jesus didn’t say go spend some more time in purgatory and work your way out.” The declaration: he released the room by name from guilt and from “the jail that church has tried to put you in” — “I don’t care what you did, but I know what you can do.” Second half — embracing the calling: Rom 1:16, not ashamed of the gospel, for it is the power of God for salvation, to the Jew first and also to the Greek. From there he moved into what he framed as possibly prophetic: to anyone who felt God’s finger on them young — at a grandparent’s church, a church camp, a worship experience — and then life happened and they concluded they had forfeited it. His own testimony, given plainly: he spent a couple of years in college not doing what he was supposed to, and “I’m standing in front of you right now as a divorced man, been remarried, and somehow I’m pastoring a church. There’s a lot of denominations I would not be allowed to do that.” He immediately guarded it — “I don’t set myself up as some kind of a trophy…as soon as I do that, be careful, I’m about to fall.” The ask: “get back in the race. We need you. We have a common purpose, and we need you to take your place, not to stay back in the shadows.” For anyone who lost their voice through what they did: this is a place to find your voice again — with God, with family, in the community. Close: Christ took on the weight of all former mistakes and released us into new life; the old is gone. Invitation to stand and acknowledge “I’m not going to live in my past anymore” — framed as “I’m not trying to call anybody out. I’m trying to call you up.” People stood; the room was asked to get up and encourage them. Benediction drew on 1 Cor 2:9 — no eye has seen, no ear has heard, nor has it entered the heart of man what God has prepared. Doctrinal read — where this landed well: the conviction/condemnation distinction was held cleanly, and the sequence he gave (repent → move forward → move on) keeps repentance intact rather than dissolving sin into self-acceptance. He conceded twice that consequences for bad decisions are real and just, which is the guardrail usually missing from “no condemnation” messages. The Paul and Peter cases are the right texts for restoration-to-ministry, and using his own divorce as the example rather than a stranger’s kept it from being cheap. Logged as a sound treatment. Translation notes — two, both the same category of drift flagged in entries #20 and #22: (1) Phil 3:13–14 was quoted as “forgetting all that lies behind, I press on toward the high calling which is in Christ Jesus” — “high calling” is the KJV; NASB 1995 reads “I press on toward the goal for the prize of the upward call of God in Christ Jesus.” (2) Rom 1:16 was quoted as “the power unto salvation…first for the Jews and then to the Gentiles” — again KJV cadence; NASB 1995 reads “the power of God for salvation to everyone who believes, to the Jew first and also to the Greek.” Pull both fresh from e-Sword if either is ever blockquoted from this entry. Honesty note — the 2 Cor 5:17 conflation: the closing line was “the old is gone, and He has made all things new.” That fuses two verses. 2 Cor 5:17 (NASB 1995) reads “the old things passed away; behold, new things have come.” “Behold, I am making all things new” is Rev 21:5, which is spoken of the new heaven and new earth, not of individual conversion. Both are true and the pastoral point survives, but they are not one verse, and the Revelation half belongs to the eternal state — worth keeping straight given how much Theme 1 work sits on Rev 21. Honesty note — Esau’s birthright vs. his blessing: the message said Esau “gave away his blessing for one bowl of soup.” In Genesis these are two separate transactions — the birthright was sold for the stew (Gen 25:29–34) and the blessing was taken later by Jacob’s deception (Gen 27). The preacher is not inventing the link: Heb 12:16–17 itself ties them together, calling Esau godless for selling his birthright and then noting he found no place for repentance when he sought the blessing with tears. Fair conclusion, imprecise wording. Honesty note — “Paul wrote almost the whole New Testament”: thirteen of twenty-seven books, and by word count less than Luke alone contributed with the Gospel and Acts. Standard pulpit shorthand, not worth a correction from the platform, but do not carry the figure into a study page. Cross-reference candidates: Theme 1 End Times — the 1 Cor 3:12–15 material is Bema Seat doctrine and maps directly onto the Phase 2 checklist item (“Bema Seat — believers’ works judged; rewards given”, 2 Cor 5:10; 1 Cor 3:11–15); this two-week series has effectively preached that checklist line, and the notes belong in that row. Theme 6 Holy Spirit — conviction vs. condemnation now appears a third time (entries #19, #22, and here); that is enough recurrence to justify building the module rather than leaving it a seed. Theme 7 Kingdom of God — eternal reward as the measure of a life; pairs with the Mark 10:45 / Acts 4 cluster from Msgs 2 and 4 of the previous series. Theme 4 Covenant — restoration of the disqualified (Paul, Peter) as new-covenant practice. Follow-up: the prepared sermon he deferred is scheduled for next Sunday, Sept 20 — worth catching, since he said the research was already finished and called the subject “very deep, powerful, and true.” Verses to pull in NASB 1995: 1 Cor 3:12–15; Gen 25:29–34; Rom 8:1; Phil 3:13–14; 2 Cor 5:17; John 21:15–17; Rom 1:16; 1 Cor 2:9; Heb 12:16–17.
#24 September 6, 2026

Possessing Eternity (A Common Purpose, Msg 1) — partial recording

Pastor Matt Scully, Trinity Church
⚠ Partial recording — the message body was not captured. I left the service early that morning because I was not feeling well, and the recording stops about twelve minutes in, mid-sentence, as Pastor Matt begins the Arthur Stace illustration. What follows is everything that was recorded: the series framing and the opening of the sermon up to the point it cuts off. For the body of “Possessing Eternity” — 1 Cor 3:12–15, the gold-and-silver versus wood-and-straw material, and the eternal filter — see the recap Pastor Matt gave the following Sunday, logged in entry #25. That recap is currently the only record of this message’s content. Series announcement: this is Msg 1 of a four-part series he is calling Common Purpose. Definition given: a common purpose is something a group shares together. Common purpose in the family: shared DNA, a shared name, shared memories, shared heritage and identity. Illustration: a few weeks earlier he drove to Conway to watch his daughter Ellie play volleyball, looked over at a crowded concession stand and saw a cousin he had not seen in four years — jumped up, hugged her neck, took a group picture and sent it to his parents. The point: the stand was full of people, but that one was family, and that made it different. Extended to the household — a home works when everyone contributes out of their own calling. “In your house, it’s not good if you have freeloaders.” Common purpose at work: a team has to pull together for the business to succeed, because an unsuccessful business cannot write anyone a paycheck. One person coasting drags the morale of the whole team, and others start matching the poor work ethic. Common purpose in school and on a team: back to Ellie — on a volleyball team the passer feeds the setter and the setter feeds the hitter; a bad pass means the setter cannot do her job, which means the hitter cannot do hers, and the point is lost because one person did not do what she was supposed to do. “Everybody coming together, so that you can be the best version of yourself.” The push against isolation — worth recording: “There’s so much scripture about how we’re to come together with a common purpose. There’s no scriptures about don’t do anything with anybody else.” He repeated it deliberately: there is a real push in the culture right now that says isolate yourself — he wondered aloud whether it is a residue of COVID — and it has come into the church as “I don’t need this group of people.” His verdict: that is not biblical. The questions he put to Trinity — the heart of what was recorded: what is our common purpose as a church, why do we exist, and what are we trying to accomplish together? He made the room sit in silence with it. Then he pressed: is our sole purpose to have a place to worship from 10:30 to noon on a Sunday — “a place to sit, sing some songs, hear a sermon, check the God box, and go back to life”? Did God put Trinity in the middle of Hot Springs for something bigger than a worship service, and did He put you in it for something bigger than yourself and bigger than your own address? Possibilities he floated as answers: helping two hundred twenty pastors, feeding twenty thousand people a year out of that building, bringing the lost in to know Christ. Note: these were offered as the church’s actual reach and are worth confirming against Trinity’s own reporting before being cited as figures anywhere on this site. 1 Pet 2:5 — living stones: Jesus named Peter a rock, which he glossed as a fragment or pebble of rock; Peter then writes that we are all living stones being fitted together as a dwelling for the Lord. Coming together to create and to fulfill a purpose. The turn into the sermon proper: “Today I really want to focus on one word, and that word is eternity…the title of today’s sermon is Possessing Eternity.” Book recommendation: John Bevere, Driven by Eternity — he said he read it about fifteen years ago and it changed both how he lives and the focus of his ministry, and that he had just recommended it to someone who asked. Arthur Stace — where the recording ends: he opened the Bevere illustration of a homeless alcoholic in Sydney, Australia named Arthur Stace who encountered Christ and then went through the city writing one word — eternity — everywhere, rising each morning to do it. The recording cuts off in the middle of this. Verification note — the Stace story checks out, with one compression: Arthur Stace (1885–1967) was a Sydney derelict and alcoholic who converted at St Barnabas’ Anglican Church, Broadway, on 6 August 1930 under R.B.S. Hammond. The chalking did not begin at his conversion, though — it began more than two years later, in November 1932, after he heard the evangelist John Ridley say he wished he could shout the word eternity through the streets of Sydney. Stace took that as a personal commission, found chalk in his pocket, and wrote the word in copperplate script on footpaths and doorsteps for roughly thirty-five years until his death; the commonly cited figure is around half a million times, which is an estimate rather than a count. His identity stayed a mystery until 1956. So the illustration is sound; the only wobble in the retelling is collapsing two separate events two years apart into one, and the moment that actually launched it was a preacher’s frustrated wish, which arguably makes the story better rather than worse. Honesty note — the living stones quotation: the line as given, “living stones, being fitted together for an habitation for the Lord,” blends two passages. 1 Pet 2:5 (NASB 1995) reads that we “are being built up as a spiritual house”; “being fitted together…into a dwelling of God in the Spirit” is Eph 2:21–22, and “habitation” is KJV vocabulary. The two texts do teach the same thing, but pull them separately in NASB 1995 if either is ever blockquoted. Same conflation category as the 2 Cor 5:17 / Rev 21:5 note in entry #25. Honesty note — Peter as “a pebble”: the gloss that Petros means a small fragment of rock as against petra, the bedrock, is a very common Protestant reading of Matt 16:18, but it is contested rather than settled. In Koine the two words were frequently used interchangeably, and Jesus almost certainly spoke Aramaic, where Kepha carries no such distinction. Nothing in this message rests on it, but the point should not be repeated as a plain fact of the Greek without the qualification. Cross-reference candidates: Theme 7 Kingdom of God — the “why does this church exist” questions are a better teaching frame than most ecclesiology material, and they pair with the Acts 4:32–35 cluster already flagged from entry #23. Theme 1 End Times — whatever else “Possessing Eternity” contained, it was Bema Seat material (see entry #25). Verses to pull in NASB 1995: 1 Pet 2:5; Eph 2:21–22; Matt 16:18.
#23 August 23, 2026

Healthy Homes Give (For God & Family, Msg 4)

Pastor Matt Scully, with Pastor Jim (founding pastor), Trinity Church
Fourth message in the For God & Family series, August theme “Healthy Homes.” Big idea: a healthy home — both the household and the church house — is a home that gives. Pastor Matt opened by telling the room to turn off their noon alarms: “the word today is it’s not going to fit within twenty-five minutes.” Ran 70 minutes. Series recap given from the pulpit: week 1 — homes that love; week 2 — homes that serve (the foot-washing service); week 3 — Chase Campbell on the fruit and evidence of a life in Christ (“Prove It”); week 4 — homes that give. Opening video — Josh Howerton: the story of Mordecai Ham’s 1934 Charlotte revival and the unnamed usher who chased down two boys who couldn’t find seats — Billy Graham and Grady Wilson. Punchline: “your job is not to change the world; your job is to change somebody’s world…run into the breach and say, not on our watch.” Applied to ushers, greeters, children’s workers, worship team. See honesty note below. Part 1 — what we give one another: children give honor to parents (Eph 6:2–3; honor looks different at 5 than at 40 — obedience, speaking well of, helping, general good relationship); wives give respect to husbands (Eph 5:33 — “I was looking for something in there that gave you the out, I can’t find it”); husbands give love to wives (Eph 5:25, as Christ loved the church and gave Himself up); believers give preference to one another (Rom 12:10). Luke 6:38 — the measure you give is the measure returned; his gloss: “sometimes we get a little bit stingy because we’re afraid if we give, we’re not going to get it back.” Rom 13:8 as the root of all four — owe nothing to anyone except to love one another. “We are indebted to each other in love.” Pastoral preface — unusual and worth recording: before turning to money he repented to the congregation, saying that in twelve years of pastoring this was only his fourth time preaching on giving and “that’s on me.” He then named the abuse directly: pastors who beat the pulpit and tell widows to surrender their pensions to buy mansions, cars, planes and helicopters, “and defame the name of God in the process. And it makes me sick.” Told the room to check him against Scripture rather than take his word: “Don’t just take what I say without checking it through scripture. Pray over it and ask the Lord to reveal to you.” Old covenant framework: Mal 3:10 — bring the whole tithe into the storehouse; the word rendered “tithe” means a tenth. Lev 27:30 — all the tithe of the land is the LORD’s, holy to the LORD. Mal 3:8–11 — robbing God, the curse, the windows of heaven, the devourer rebuked. Four stated purposes of the Old Covenant tithe: (1) an act of worship — a holy portion set aside and given back; (2) provision for the Levites, the one tribe given no land, whose work was the house of the LORD; (3) care for the community — the poor, the stranger, the fatherless, the widow, the hungry; (4) gratitude and acknowledgment of God’s lordship over money, family, and job. Quoted approvingly: “the last thing that gets saved in a person’s life is their wallet.” Object lesson: a bag advertised as eleven apples that actually held ten — God saying keep nine, hand one back, and the one handed back feeds the priest, the widow, the fatherless. Old-to-new covenant method: his controlling analogy was sacrifice — daily under the old covenant, not abolished under the new but fulfilled in Christ. Second example: prophecy as correction/warning/judgment in the OT, defined by Paul as edification, exhortation and consolation in the NT. Applied to giving: the practice does not vanish, the heart changes. His illustration: “I am going to feed my children today so that I don’t go to jail” versus “I am going to feed my children today because I love them.” Old covenant fear-based; new covenant love-based. Explicitly answered the question he had raised: does the new covenant mean we keep all ten apples? “No. That’s not what it means. But the heart behind it changed.” New covenant texts: Matt 23:23 — woe to the scribes and Pharisees who tithe mint and dill and cumin but neglect justice, mercy and faithfulness; “these are the things you should have done without neglecting the others.” His paraphrase: “don’t tithe and then be ugly to people — but don’t be nice to people and not tithe. It’s both.” Acts 4:32–35 — the early church held nothing as its own, laid proceeds at the apostles’ feet, and distributed to each as any had need; “there was not a needy person among them.” His read: not ten percent but everything available, motivated by love rather than fear — “so we’re giving you a discount today.” 2 Cor 9:5–7 treated as the key that unlocks the subject, with background: Corinth had promised support for Jerusalem and Paul wrote ahead so the gift would be ready and not extracted under pressure. Three words drawn from v.7: (1) compulsion — and here he disclosed a policy decision: Trinity stopped passing the plate at COVID and never resumed; giving is by wall boxes, mail, or app. Someone told him to bring back the plate “because it’ll make people feel guilty to where they want to give more” and he refused. Cited as a counterexample a preacher who told a congregation that Christ had not returned yet and “it’s your fault” for not funding his ministry. (2) begrudgingly — not “oh, this one’s going to hurt.” (3) as he has purposed in his heart — have you asked God what He wants of you, and are you obeying it? The motive test: he named the prosperity framing as the thing that breaks people — “one of the ways that I think pastors try to get you to tithe is they make giving about you.” His position: God does rebuke the devourer and does bless, “because He said He promised to do it,” but that is not the motivation. “Whenever we make the tithe about us, we write the check, something bad happens, and we’re like, see, it doesn’t work.” Three reasons he gives: (1) identity — Mark 10:45, the Son of Man came not to be served but to serve and give His life a ransom for many; “Christ gave and I am made in His image.” (2) obedience — firstfruits; he and Lindsay pay the full month’s tithe on the first paycheck and live on the rest. (3) love — “at some point you are going to call here and just say, pastor, I need some help. And because I love you, I want to say yes,” plus Myanmar, Vietnam, Mexico, Texas, and Hot Springs. Personal testimony — Matt and Pastor Jim: early on at Trinity he made $400 a week as his household’s only earner; benefits changed, medical bills came due, and he decided to redirect his tithe to pay them — “seems like an honest and noble thing for your family.” Jim, reviewing the giving records, called him over in the fellowship hall and asked gently what was wrong. Jim’s answer: pay your tithe first, and if you come up short, come see me and I’ll help you. Matt never missed a tithe again and never once had to ask. He was careful with the full story: there were times Jim and his parents offered help he had not asked for, so it was not a windfall — “the Lord always met the need.” He also credited Jim for being hard on him: “you could take the easy way with me, but you bothered me. So thank you.” Why Jim spoke: Jim was woken in the night a couple of days earlier with a word for the church about the tithe and came down during worship to tell Matt, who told him he was already preaching on it that morning. Jim: “that’s the word of the Lord.” The family meeting — church finances: anchored on Hag 1:4, the same verse used in Msg 1 and Msg 2 (“is it time for you yourselves to dwell in your paneled houses while my house lies desolate?”). Stated figures: typical Sunday attendance ~300, ~500 who consider themselves regular members, 460 chairs set out. Church insurance $46,000 per year, saved toward all year and paid a couple of weeks ago. HVAC in the preschool building failed this week — a freon leak, $4,000, fixed (he thanked Shane). For the first time in twelve years of pastoring, there was not enough to pay the pastors and staff that week; the paychecks came out of savings. $60,000 pulled from savings year to date to stay afloat. His framing: “I have zero fear…if the Lord one day is like, hey Trinity, your day is over, then praise the Lord, it was a good run. But that day is not today. But we can’t keep living like this.” Invoked Abraham on the mountain — the Lord provides, but “we have to be obedient in our part of that story.” Noted twice that the message was written three months ago, before the shortfall. The benevolence case — the part he said actually got him: a family in the church whose child went into liver failure and received a transplant; wife and child living in St. Louis for the recovery and medications while the husband stayed in Hot Springs to keep his job. Weeks ago the wife was diagnosed with bowel cancer, and this week had a severe reaction to chemotherapy and was rushed to the hospital. The husband called the church for help and Matt had to tell him there was no money. “That is not who we are. Whenever one of our own is in that place, we should be ready to give.” Missions declined: Myanmar missionaries in a civil war with Christians being killed asked for support toward a children’s crusade — declined, nothing in the account. Vietnam asked — declined. The ask: not a one-time appeal but consistent obedient giving; “I am not putting you under the law, but I am putting you under the heart of what Christ has asked.” To those who say they have nothing to give, he repeated Jim’s words over him: be faithful, and if you need help, let us know. Pastor Jim’s exhortation (~57:00 to close): Trinity began when a man Jim had never met, who said he was a prophet — “I had my doubts about somebody being a prophet” — told him God would raise up and build the church, it would not cost a dime, and streams from it would touch nations of the earth. Jim says the property was built debt-free because he was told never to go into debt, since the money was to go to the poor, the needy, the suffering, and the children of the world — orphanages, a hospital, schools. The man who taught him to “live to give” was a missionary who had planted more churches in Mexico than anyone; he spoke at Jim’s church about congregations meeting without roofs and pastors without bicycles, then pledged on Jim’s behalf — a thousand dollars, then another thousand, up to five thousand. “I didn’t have five hundred dollars.” Jim admits he hoped the man would never come back. Mozambique: Ezra and Eli wired on a Sunday morning that children were dying from eating poisonous roots. Jim, who hated raising money, floundered from the platform — “mine was a shipwreck…sinking like the Titanic” — and the same missionary walked in uninvited and unannounced, sat on the front row, endured about five minutes of it, then said “why don’t you sit down, let me do this,” and raised thousands that morning. The money bought grain; sixteen thousand fed, three thousand professions of faith, and two hundred seventy churches in Mozambique that Jim traces to that giving. His thesis: “this is not about just tithe. It’s about living to give, and you give out of a love for the one that gave it all.” He then gave the hundred dollars in his pocket toward the family in crisis, saying he wanted nothing back from God. Closed with his own testimony: thirteen years without children; he obeyed the call to start the church “not out of compulsion, but out of just wanting to do what God wanted me to do,” and his wife conceived that month. Seven grandchildren now. Final word, and notable for how carefully it was framed: “You just ask God what He would have you to do, and I guarantee you the windows of heaven will open up — not because you tithed, but because you wanted to bless the heart of God.” Doctrinal read — where this landed well: both men kept Malachi’s curse language under the Old Covenant heading rather than transferring it onto believers, which is the usual failure point in tithing sermons. Matt explicitly rejected guilt mechanics (the plate decision), explicitly rejected the transactional prosperity frame while still affirming God’s promise to provide, and told the congregation to test him against Scripture. Jim detached the blessing from the transaction in his final sentence. Logged here as a sound treatment. Open exegetical question worth a future study: the message assumed the tithe carries forward into the New Covenant as a floor, resting the case on Matt 23:23 (“without neglecting the others”) and on the Acts 4 pattern of proportional-to-total giving. That is a defensible position and the most common one, but it is contested — Matt 23:23 addresses Jews still under the law before the cross, and the NT epistles consistently teach proportional, purposed, cheerful giving (2 Cor 8:12; 2 Cor 9:7; 1 Cor 16:2) without ever restating a ten-percent figure. Matt himself acknowledged the disagreement exists (“that’s one of the big arguments…in modern day Christendom”). Flagged not as an error but as the live question underneath the message, and a good candidate for a study that lays out both readings from the text. Honesty note — the opening video: the historical core is accurate — Mordecai Ham held a lengthy revival in Charlotte in 1934, and Billy Graham and Grady Wilson both came forward there; Wilson went on to become Graham’s first associate evangelist and BGEA vice president. Three details in the retelling do not hold up. The boys were fifteen (Graham turned sixteen that November; Wilson was born August 1919), not fourteen. Graham’s own autobiographical account describes attending repeatedly over weeks at his parents’ urging and joining the choir to hide from Ham’s gaze — not wandering past, finding no seats, and turning away. And the usher who allegedly chased them down does not appear in Graham’s account; the story circulates widely in devotional form (Os Hillman among others) with the usher described as “unknown to this day,” which is another way of saying unsourced. The venue was also a purpose-built wooden tabernacle with a sawdust floor rather than a canvas tent. The point the video makes — that unnamed faithfulness compounds — stands on its own and does not need the embellishment. Testimony vs. doctrine: Jim’s founding narrative (the unnamed prophet’s word that the church would be built without cost) and the Mozambique figures are recorded here as his personal testimony, not as verified fact or as a normative promise. He tells them as history, not as a formula, and they should be read that way. Opening sermon of new series “Living in Time.” Core text: Eccl 3:1 — “There is an appointed time for everything.” Big idea: God would rather we be IN His time than ON time. Pastor's Sonic drive-thru story — late to a 9:30 appointment because of a 10-minute parking-lot conversation with a church member; that conversation was where God's anointing landed that morning. Five biblical case studies of God being “in time” rather than “on time”: (1) Gal 4:4 — “the fullness of time” / “just the right time”; Christ came at the appointed moment, even though Moses, David, Elijah, Daniel might all have wished He'd come in their day (Matt 13:17). (2) John 11 / Lazarus — Jesus deliberately stayed two more days because He loved them (the “therefore” of v.6); not on time for the healing, but in time for the resurrection; Martha's model faith in v.22 (“but even now, I know that whatever You ask of God, God will give You”). (3) Luke 8 / Jairus & the woman with the issue of blood — Jesus stops to heal the bleeding woman while Jairus's daughter dies; not on time for the healing, but in time for the raising (Luke 8:54–55: “Child, arise”). (4) Matt 14 — Jesus grieving John the Baptist's beheading wanted solitude; compassion moved Him to be in time for the multitudes anyway. (5) Acts 8 / Philip and the Ethiopian eunuch — pulled out of a citywide revival to meet one man on a desert road; that one conversation carried the gospel to a whole nation. Counter-warning: 1 Sam 13 / Saul at Gilgal — Saul waited the seven days but ran out of patience and offered the burnt offering himself; cost him a dynasty that would have continued in Christ's lineage. Right thing, wrong time. Discernment text: 1 Chr 12:32 — the sons of Issachar, “men who understood the times, with knowledge of what Israel should do.” Closing text: Ps 31:15 — “My times are in Your hand.” Personal testimony: Pastor Matt's 20-year vision of pastors meeting in this sanctuary; 5,000-person Easter 2026 service at his alma mater football field with five Hot Springs pastors fulfilling Eph 4 fivefold pattern (Trinity, Lake Point, Lakeview, New Life, Freedom). Pastor Matt's prayer request for the church: financial board meeting next Sun, elders the following Tues, on a major decision for Trinity Church — pray that they discern God's timing. Personal connection: same passage (John 11) the Lord put in front of me this morning in e-Sword before service. Cross-reference candidates: Theme 1 (the “why hasn't He come yet” question; 2 Pet 3:9; Matt 24:32–33 fig tree); Theme 2 (Gal 4:4 “fullness of time” as a moedim/appointed-time statement); Theme 3 (Saul's impatience vs. God's 120-year patience in the days of Noah, 1 Pet 3:20).
